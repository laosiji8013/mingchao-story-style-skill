# Style Units

Each unit is distilled as a transferable mechanism. Do not copy source phrasing.

## 1. Reader-Side Narrator

- R: The narrator stands beside the reader and explains events as if walking through a case together.
- I: The voice is informed but conversational. It lowers distance without lowering standards.
- A1: Makes historical material feel approachable.
- A2: Use when explaining AI, products, procedures, or institutions to non-specialists.
- E: Address the reader's likely confusion, then guide them through the key facts.
- B: Do not become chatty filler. The narrator still serves the facts.

## 2. Fact Anchor Before Performance

- R: Even when the prose is lively, it keeps returning to concrete names, dates, roles, actions, or institutional facts.
- I: Entertainment is built on top of information, not instead of information.
- A1: Keeps story-like writing from becoming fabrication.
- A2: Use when turning technical or business material into readable content.
- E: Name the fact first, explain what it means, then add interpretation.
- B: If facts are missing, state the gap instead of smoothing it over.

## 3. Short-Paragraph Staircase

- R: The prose advances through many short paragraphs rather than long blocks.
- I: Each paragraph functions like one step in reasoning or plot movement.
- A1: Makes long chronology easy to follow.
- A2: Use for any dense material that readers might otherwise skim.
- E: Keep most paragraphs around 20-60 Chinese characters; one point per paragraph; let transitions carry movement.
- B: Do not chop every sentence mechanically. Group tightly related facts.

## 4. Judgment First, Explanation After

- R: The narrator often gives a clear judgment, then explains why the judgment holds.
- I: The reader gets orientation before detail.
- A1: Prevents the narrative from becoming a list of facts.
- A2: Use when readers need to know "what this means" before "how it works."
- E: Start sections with "关键不在 X，而在 Y" or an equivalent original judgment, then prove it.
- B: Avoid unsupported certainty.

## 5. Turn-Driven Movement

- R: Reversals and contrast words appear frequently.
- I: The style moves by "things looked like this, but actually that happened."
- A1: Converts chronology into tension.
- A2: Use for product gaps, process failures, market changes, and historical reversals.
- E: Use "然而/不过/可是/但是/问题是" when the reader's expectation should change.
- B: Do not manufacture drama when the facts are stable.

## 6. Chronology With Causality

- R: Time movement is tied to cause and consequence.
- I: "Then" is not enough; each step needs a reason or pressure.
- A1: Turns event sequence into story logic.
- A2: Use for workflows, incident reviews, project histories, and biographies.
- E: For each step, answer: who acted, what pressure existed, what changed afterward.
- B: Do not list timestamps without explaining why they matter.

## 7. Plain-Language Translation

- R: Abstract systems are explained through ordinary language and concrete roles.
- I: A difficult term is not accepted until it has been translated.
- A1: Lets non-specialists follow political, military, and institutional material.
- A2: Use for AI, product, compliance, operations, and equipment concepts.
- E: Use "所谓 X，就是..." internally, then polish into natural prose.
- B: Do not oversimplify away important limitations.

## 8. Character-as-Pressure Model

- R: People are presented through background, desire, limitation, and pressure.
- I: Character is not just personality; it is a person under constraints.
- A1: Makes figures memorable without turning them into pure fiction.
- A2: Use for founders, product users, operators, managers, or historical figures.
- E: Describe origin, role, goal, constraint, choice, consequence.
- B: Avoid psychological claims without evidence.

## 9. Parenthetical Aside

- R: Parentheses and brief asides lower tension and create narrator presence.
- I: The aside is a controlled wink, not the main argument.
- A1: Keeps serious material readable.
- A2: Use sparingly in public-facing explainers.
- E: Add a short aside after a factual statement when it clarifies tone.
- B: Remove asides in formal, legal, safety, or sensitive contexts.

## 10. Clean Local Closure

- R: Sections often end by closing the local point clearly before moving on.
- I: The reader should know what just changed.
- A1: Helps long narratives stay legible.
- A2: Use for section endings, product judgments, and process summaries.
- E: End with a sentence that answers "so what?"
- B: Avoid slogans and inflated endings.
